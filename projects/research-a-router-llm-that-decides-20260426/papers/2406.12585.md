###### Abstract

Ensembling multiple models has always been an effective approach to push the limits of existing performance and is widely used in classification tasks by simply averaging the classification probability vectors from multiple classifiers to achieve better accuracy. However, in the thriving open-source Large Language Model (LLM) community, ensembling methods are rare and typically limited to ensembling the full-text outputs of LLMs, such as selecting the best output using a ranker, which leads to underutilization of token-level probability information. In this paper, we treat the G eneration of each token by LLMs a s a C lassification (GaC) for ensembling. This approach fully exploits the probability information at each generation step and better prevents LLMs from producing early incorrect tokens that lead to snowballing errors. In experiments, we ensemble state-of-the-art LLMs on several benchmarks, including exams, mathematics and reasoning, and observe that our method breaks the existing community performance ceiling. Furthermore, we observed that most of the tokens in the answer are simple and do not affect the correctness of the final answer. Therefore, we also experimented with ensembling only key tokens, and the results showed better performance with lower latency across benchmarks.1 1 1 Our code: [https://github.com/yaoching0/GaC](https://github.com/yaoching0/GaC)

1 Introduction
--------------

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in a wide range of natural language processing tasks (Achiam et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib2); Touvron et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib45)). Over time, new and more powerful LLMs are continually being released, pushing the boundaries of the LLM community (Meta, [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib33); Alibaba, [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib3)). Due to the diversity of data sources, architectures and training methods, different LLMs have strengths and weaknesses in different tasks and contexts (Jiang et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib24)). In addition to investing significant resources in training a superior LLM, ensembling multiple existing models is another effective way to break through the community performance ceiling (Huang et al., [2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib19)), especially given the current trend in the open source LLM community to contribute only model weights rather than training data and procedures (AllenAI, [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib4)).

![Image 1: Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2/x1.png)

Figure 1: Motivation of GaC. The upper part shows CV classification ensemble, while the lower part illustrates ensemble at one text generation step.

Taking computer vision (CV) classification as an example, it is common to ensemble the output probability vectors of multiple models (e.g. by averaging) to achieve superior results (Krizhevsky et al., [2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib28)). This approach remains effective even with recent CV models. As shown in Tab.[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling"), we selected several common CV models (Chen et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib10); Tan and Le, [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib44); Wang et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib50)) for ensembling and observed better accuracy on ImageNet (Deng et al., [2009](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib14)) compared to using a single model. Similarly, the popular decoder-only LLM architecture generates text by producing tokens one by one, with each generation step resulting in a probability vector of the length of the vocabulary. Inspired by CV, we propose to treat each G eneration step a s a C lassification task (GaC), and by ensembling multiple models, we can achieve higher accuracy, as shown in Fig.[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling"). There is already work that simplifies problems into binary tasks, exploiting the collective wisdom of LLMs and achieving better results (Schoenegger et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib40)), demonstrating the feasibility of this approach.

Table 1: Performance of various CV models on ImageNet and LLMs on MMLU. ↑↑\uparrow↑ indicates improvement over a single model.

Another advantage is that early errors in LLMs often snowball into later errors (Zhang et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib56)). Ensembling during generation helps prevent the generation of inaccurate tokens at each step, thereby reducing misleading cues for subsequent token generation. In this paper, we conducted experiments at several points in time between November 2023 and June 2024, ensembling available state-of-the-art (SOTA) LLMs up to each of these points. We found that this approach significantly outperformed any single model available at those times as well as existing methods on five popular benchmarks involving subject examination, mathematics, reasoning, and knowledge-based QA.

In addition, we found that for text generation it seemed unnecessary to ensemble at every step. For example, for the question "What Andean animal has banana-shaped ears?" shown in Fig.[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling"), the most critical part is for the LLM to generate the key token "llama". The initial part of the answer "It should be _" or "The animal is _" do not significantly affect the correctness of the final answer. Ideally, the step that produces the token "llama" is the one we want to ensemble.

Studies in CV classification have also shown that most samples are "simple" and can be correctly classified by most models (Wang et al., [2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib51)), including cost-efficient ones, making the use of expensive models wasteful. To address this, CV classification used cascade inference Jazbec et al. ([2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib22)); Enomoro and Eda ([2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib15)), where a gate model passes a sample to a more powerful model only if its confidence falls below a threshold, thereby improving efficiency. Obviously, it is very important for cascading that the confidence of the gate model accurately reflects the accuracy. To ensure that LLMs are also suitable as gate models, we measured the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) (Guo et al., [2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib17)) of CV models and LLMs on ImageNet and MMLU (Hendrycks et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib18)), as shown in Tab.[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling"). ECE is a metric that reflects the difference between a model’s confidence and its accuracy. We found that the ECE of CV models and LLMs were close. Therefore, in this paper, we also applied the cascade inference to LLMs by ensembling only the "key" tokens to speed up generation. Our experiments showed that this approach consistently achieved better performance with lower latency across different benchmarks.

In summary, we propose a framework GaC, which allows multiple heterogeneous LLMs to jointly decide the next generated token during text generation:

*   •When we ensemble at every generation step, experiments show that it outperforms any single SOTA LLM from different time periods and existing ensemble methods. 
*   •By using cascade to ensemble only at important steps during text generation, experiments show better results with lower latency. 

2 Analysis and Prior Work
-------------------------

In this chapter, we review previous LLM ensemble studies and their features, as well as the problems our approach addresses. Previous studies can be categorized as follows:

Output-level ensemble methods select multiple candidate models and use their complete outputs for ensembling. Jiang et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib24)) trained an additional ranking model (_PairRanker_) to score each candidate output and select the best one. Lu et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib31)) and Shnitzer et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib42)) trained a router to select the most appropriate candidate model given a question. However, these methods are limited to the existing candidate outputs and become ineffective if all the outputs are incorrect. Other studies have trained a fusion model to blend the outputs (Jiang et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib24); Wang et al., [2023b](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib49)), overcoming the limitation of selecting only existing candidate outputs and often achieving superior results. However, the generalization of the fusion model is a major challenge, and they cannot fully exploit the probability information from each generation step.

Weight-level ensemble methods merge the weights of multiple models and are primarily used in multi-task learning (Yadav et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib53)). The expectation is that the merged model will inherit capabilities across multiple tasks. However, a limitation is that the architectures of the models to be merged must be homologous, which limits the use of the capabilities of the LLM community. And it is rare to observe that the merged model outperforms the original models (Yu et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib55)).

Training-level ensemble like FuseLLM (Wan et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib46)) uses the output probability vectors of multiple models during training to ensemble as labels, rather than one-hot labels. In effect, this is a specific form of distillation that allows the model being trained to gain more information from the probability outputs of the ensembled (teacher) models. However, distillation is mainly used to improve small models, making it difficult to further improve the SOTA LLMs.

Our work can overcome the above limitations by ensembling at each generation step, allowing the output not to be confined to the original candidate output space and homologous architectures, while fully exploiting the probability information at each step. Our experiments in Sec.[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling") will also show that the ensemble consistently outperforms any single model, even SOTA LLMs. The main challenge, however, is that different LLMs typically have inconsistent vocabularies, leading to different dimensions in the probability vectors produced by different models. The most intuitive solution is to take the union of the vocabularies of the ensembled LLMs, denoted V U superscript 𝑉 𝑈 V^{U}italic_V start_POSTSUPERSCRIPT italic_U end_POSTSUPERSCRIPT, which includes all tokens from the participating models. Then, at each generation step, the output is first mapped to this union space ℝ|V U|superscript ℝ superscript 𝑉 𝑈\mathbb{R}^{|V^{U}|}blackboard_R start_POSTSUPERSCRIPT | italic_V start_POSTSUPERSCRIPT italic_U end_POSTSUPERSCRIPT | end_POSTSUPERSCRIPT before ensembling.

A potential problem with this approach is that different models may tokenize the same word differently, leading to conflicts. However, most mainstream LLMs use BPE or BBPE (Sennrich et al., [2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib41); Wang et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib47)) to train tokenizers on sampled corpora, which tend to have similar sources (e.g. CommonCrawl) and distributions. This results in consistent tokenization for common words. For example, both Qwen1.5 and Llama3 (Bai et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib6); Meta, [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib33)) tokenize the word "_alphabetically_" into ["_Ġalphabet_", "_ically_"]. If both models intend to output this word, they will assign a higher probability to "_Ġalphabet_" first. We selected several popular LLMs (Young et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib54); Databricks, [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib13); Almazrouei et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib5)) and tokenized 5,000 commonly used English words (Oxford, [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib38)), and then calculated the proportion of identical tokenization results between each pair of LLMs, as shown in Fig.[2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Analysis and Prior Work ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling"). The proportion is above 90%percent 90 90\%90 % for all pairs, indicating that such conflicts can be ignored in most cases.

### 3.1 Overall Process of GaC

When generating text, LLMs output a probability vector of the same dimension as their vocabulary. Given n 𝑛 n italic_n LLMs to be ensembled, we first take the union of their vocabularies and create a mapping matrix that can project the probability vectors to the union dimensions (Sec.[3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S3.SS2 "3.2 Creating the Union Mapping ‣ 3 Proposed Method ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling")). At each generation step, all LLMs produce outputs that are mapped to the union vocabulary dimensions and ensembled to sample the next token. The tokenizer of each LLM then converts the sampled token into token IDs for the next step (Sec.[3.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S3.SS3 "3.3 GaC Ensembling ‣ 3 Proposed Method ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling")). As mentioned in Sec.[1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling"), not all generation steps have the necessity for ensembling, so we also try to ensemble only certain key tokens (Sec.[3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S3.SS4 "3.4 Ensembling Key Tokens with Threshold ‣ 3 Proposed Method ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling")).

### 4.1 Overview

### 4.2 Experimental Settings

Benchmarks.GaC is not limited to specific tasks, so we tested it as broadly as possible. We selected a total of five benchmarks. For general capabilities, we chose MMLU (Hendrycks et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib18)). For maths, we utilized GSM8K (Cobbe et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib11)). For reasoning, we employed BBH (Suzgun et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib43)). For knowledge capabilities, we included TriviaQA (Joshi et al., [2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib25)) and NaturalQuestions (NQ) (Kwiatkowski et al., [2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib29)). Note that all scores, including those for individual models, were computed locally under the same environment to ensure fairness 3 3 3 Please see Appx.[B](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#A2 "Appendix B Benchmarks ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling") for more benchmarks details., using lm-evaluation-harness 4 4 4[https://github.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness](https://github.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness) v0.4.1 Gao et al. Hardware and Latency. During ensembling, different LLMs were loaded on separate GPU(s) and executed in parallel, managed and communicated via Ray (Moritz et al., [2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib34)). We also recorded the latency (ms/token). Each model performs a "dry run" after being loaded onto the GPU, generating 1024 tokens to warm up CUDA before experimentation, following the practice of Mehta et al. ([2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib32)).

Baselines. We compared GaC with existing methods. First, we considered LLM Blender (Jiang et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib24)), which employs PairRanker to rank the outputs of candidate LLMs and GenFuser to fuse these outputs. However, we found that GenFuser refused to answer a significant proportion of the questions in our chosen benchmarks. We therefore only used PairRanker to ensure fairness. We also included other rankers, such as OAssistRM 6 6 6[https://huggingface.co/OpenAssistant/reward-model](https://huggingface.co/OpenAssistant/reward-model-deberta-v3-large-v2)(Köpf et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib27)) and UltraRM (Cui et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib12)), which we ran in parallel on the GPUs hosting the ensemble LLMs to ensure low latency. These rankers scored the outputs and selected the best answers. ### 4.6 Ensemble with Threshold

In this experiment, we used the thresholded ensemble (Sec.[3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S3.SS4 "3.4 Ensembling Key Tokens with Threshold ‣ 3 Proposed Method ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling")) to explore variations in latency and performance. We selected models of different sizes (Abdin et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib1); Meta, [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib33); Bai et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib6)), listed in the upper part of Tab.[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 4.5 Breaking the Ceiling ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling"), pairing a smaller model with a larger model and using the smaller model as the gate model for the ensemble. We aimed to match the performance (average score) of Qwen1.5-72B-chat and Qwen1.5-32B-chat with our ensemble, but with lower latency, and the results are shown in row ids 6-9. Interestingly, even when combining the two Qwen models themselves with a threshold of 0.5 (row id 7), where 6.31% of the tokens were ensembled, we observed slightly higher performance than Qwen1.5-72B-chat (average score increased from 60.55 to 60.96) and lower latency (102.11 to 77.86 ms/token). We believe this is a promising new way to speed up inference.

We also observed lower latency in row ids 6, 8 and 9 of Tab.[6](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 4.5 Breaking the Ceiling ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling") with comparable performance to Qwen1.5-72B-chat or Qwen1.5-32B-chat. In addition, similar trends were observed in the MT-Bench (Zheng et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib57)) using LLM as judge (GPT-4-0613) in Tab.[7](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S4.T7 "Table 7 ‣ 4.5 Breaking the Ceiling ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling"), with scores calculated using FastChat 9 9 9[https://github.com/lm-sys/FastChat](https://github.com/lm-sys/FastChat) in our local environment. For the combinations of Llama-3-8B-Instruct or Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct with Llama-3-70B-Instruct, we directly adopted the output of the larger model if the probability of the smaller model was below the threshold. This is based on our observations in Fig.[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.5 Breaking the Ceiling ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling"), where ensembling models with large performance gaps resulted in reduced performance.

5 Conclusion
------------

In this paper, we present a token-level ensembling framework called GaC, which fully exploits the probability information at each generation step. In our experiments, we have surpassed the performance ceiling of open-source SOTA LLMs available at different time periods (Sec.[4.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S4.SS5 "4.5 Breaking the Ceiling ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling")), further narrowing the gap between open-source and proprietary models. This progress promotes the democratization of LLMs and provides new motivations for future research, enabling better exploitation of collective intelligence. In addition, we experimented with ensembling just a few tokens and found that this approach can achieve better performance with lower latency (Sec.[4.6](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#S4.SS6 "4.6 Ensemble with Threshold ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Breaking the Ceiling of the LLM Community by Treating Token Generation as a Classification for Ensembling")), opening up new avenues for accelerating inference.

Contemporaneous Works
---------------------

We have noticed several contemporaneous works related to our research, all of which aim to address the vocabulary discrepancy between different models. Xu et al. ([2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib52)) proposed EVA, which trains a projection matrix between each pair of LLMs, using the overlapping tokens from their vocabularies as a bridge. DEEPEN (Huang et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12585v2#bib.bib20)) converts the output probabilities to a relative representation using anchor tokens before ensembling, and then inverts back to the original model’s vocabulary space using gradient descent, which requires an additional 7%percent 7 7\%7 % to 29%percent 29 29\%29 % of time per generation step. In contrast, our method requires no additional training and only a single matrix multiplication and tokenization for each model during ensembling, with minimal time cost.

Limitations
-----------

Like other ensemble methods, the approach proposed in this paper requires more computational resources. Although different models can be run in parallel on separate GPUs, so that latency only depends on the slowest model, the overall computational load is additive, raising the threshold for use.
